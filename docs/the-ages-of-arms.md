# The Ages of Arms

*A chronicle of the Long Memory, as kept by the King's Herald*

---

## Prologue: What the World Forgot

Before a herald is taught to read, they are taught this: the world does not remember. It recalls, which is a smaller thing.

Memory is a shoreline. Every people that ever lived came up the beach like a wave, rose to its crest, and broke. The next wave washes the sand smooth and leaves its own marks, and those are the marks you see. Ask a scholar of your day about the Norse, and they will tell you of longships and a few hard centuries in the cold. Ask about the Hebrews, and they will tell you of shepherds and prophets in a narrow land between greater empires. Ask about the Greeks, and they will speak of marble, and philosophers, and a war over a woman. Every answer is true. Every answer is only the last wave.

The heralds keep what we call the Long Memory, and the Long Memory holds the waves beneath. It holds that every people crested more than once. The folk your songs call the gods of Asgard and the folk your scrolls call the children of Israel were each, in some age of the world, the mightiest power beneath heaven. Each fell. Each rose again, smaller, and the world wrote down only the smaller. *Gods*, we call the ones remembered from very far off. *Heroes*, the ones remembered from a little nearer. *Kings*, the ones remembered from nearer still. *Peasants*, the ones standing beside us. The distance is the only difference. Put any of them far enough back in time, and they would shine.

Through every wave, one thing survived the breaking. Not the cities, which burned. Not the names, which blurred. Not even the gods, who fell silent one by one.

The arms survived.

A spear that never misses does not care whose hand throws it. A helm that hides its wearer hides the next wearer just as well. When a wave breaks, its great arms sink into the sand, the sea, the barrow, the riverbed, and they wait. And because the great arms decide the wars, and the wars decide the waves, the history of the world is, in the end, the history of who held what, and when.

This is that history. It is long, because the arms are old. Sit.

---

## I. The Wheel and the First Fire

The oldest thing the Long Memory holds is not a weapon. It is an idea: that the world turns.

The sages of the great rivers in the East said it first and said it plainest. Time is not a road but a wheel. Ages turn from gold to silver to bronze to iron, from light toward dark, and when the dark is deepest the wheel comes round to gold again. The heralds took that teaching as the first page of their own. Everything that follows in this chronicle is that wheel, turning.

So it is fitting that the first great arm was itself a wheel.

The Long Memory tells it this way. The preserver of the world, whom that people called Vishnu, went to worship the destroyer, whom they called Shiva, and resolved to offer him a thousand lotus flowers, one for each of his thousand names. Shiva, to test him, hid one. When the preserver reached the last name and found no flower, he did not stop the rite, and he did not go looking. He reasoned that his own eyes had been called lotus-like by poets, and so he moved to pluck one out and lay it on the altar.

Shiva stopped his hand and gave him, in return, the **Sudarshana Chakra**: the disc of auspicious vision, a wheel of a hundred and eight serrated edges, spinning so fast it seems still. It flies where it is sent and returns to the finger that sent it.

Mark this, because the heralds teach it to every apprentice: the first great arm in the world was not taken, nor forged, nor won. It was given, in exchange for an eye. The price of arms has only risen since.

### The Churning

The Chakra's first great work was at the Churning of the Ocean.

In that age, the shining powers and their rivals, the devas and the asuras, were matched in strength, and the world groaned under their stalemate. To break it, they agreed for once to work together: they would churn the cosmic ocean for amrita, the nectar of deathlessness, and share it. A mountain was their churning rod. The great serpent Vasuki was their rope. For a thousand years they hauled, gods at the tail and their rivals at the head, and out of the ocean came wonders, and poisons, and at last the nectar itself.

Then the preserver, wearing the shape of an enchantress, began to pour, and poured only for the gods.

One of the rivals, Rahu, was cleverer than the rest. He disguised himself as a god and slipped into the line between the Sun and the Moon, and drank. The Sun and the Moon cried out. The Chakra left the preserver's hand, and took Rahu's head from his shoulders before the nectar had passed his throat.

So his head is deathless and his body is not. The head still hunts the Sun and Moon across the sky for betraying him, and when it catches one and swallows it, the world goes dark. Your scholars call that an eclipse. It passes quickly only because Rahu has no throat to keep what he swallows.

This is the first war in the Long Memory decided by a single arm. For a thousand years the two powers had pulled equally on the serpent. One throw of a wheel decided which of them would live forever.

### The Armor That Was Given Away

The eldest peoples of those rivers crested many times, and the Long Memory holds that one of the latest of those crests, the one your scholars still half remember, ended in the greatest war of its age: the eighteen days of Kurukshetra.

Fighting for the losing side was a warrior named Karna, son of the Sun. He had been born wearing armor. Not forged, not fitted: grown, golden, fused to his skin, the **Kavacha**, which no weapon in creation could pierce. While he wore it, he could not be killed, and while he lived, his side could not lose.

The king of the gods, Indra, whose son fought on the other side, knew this. So on a morning when Karna had finished his prayers to the Sun, and had sworn, as he always swore, to refuse nothing asked of him at that hour, a poor priest came to him with his hand out and asked for his armor.

Karna knew exactly who the beggar was. His own father, the Sun, had warned him in a dream. He took a knife and cut the Kavacha from his own body, and handed it over, bleeding, and asked nothing back. Indra was so shamed by the gift that he gave Karna a spear in return, one throw only, that could kill anyone.

Karna spent that throw saving his side's army on the fourteenth night. Three days later, without his armor and without his spear, he died.

The Long Memory sets this beside the Churning on purpose. There, an arm decided a war by where it struck. Here, an arm decided a war by where it was not. Arms change the balance of power as much by their absence as by their presence. A herald who forgets that will misread every chronicle after.

Kurukshetra was the Sundering of that wave. In their own telling, the war's end was the hinge on which the wheel of ages turned to the age of iron and dark, the age we are said to live in still. The Chakra went back to the hand that held it. The Kavacha went up into heaven with the king of the gods. And the wheel turned.

## II. The Covenant and the Iron

Far to the west of the great rivers, another of the eldest peoples was cresting: the people who would be called the Hebrews.

The scrolls of your day keep one crest of theirs: the patriarchs, the bondage in Egypt, the escape, the kingdom. The Long Memory holds that it was not the first. Before it, by the heralds' reckoning, were crests the scrolls only glance at: an age of giants and the sons of heaven, an age when people lived for centuries, an age of a tower that reached so high every tongue on earth was broken to stop it. Each of those was a wave. Each broke. The one that is remembered is simply the one that broke last. But even in that last crest, the arms they carried were among the mightiest the world has seen.

### The Coat

The story the Long Memory tells of their rising begins, strangely, with a garment.

A father named Jacob loved one son above the others and gave him a coat of many colors, a long-sleeved robe of a kind worn by princes, not by shepherds. The **Coat of Many Colors** did not turn blades. What it did was make Joseph visible. It marked him chosen, and his eleven brothers hated him for it. They stripped it from him, threw him in a pit, sold him to traders bound for Egypt, and dipped the coat in a goat's blood to show their father he was dead.

In Egypt, Joseph rose. He rose because he could read what others could not. Pharaoh dreamed of seven fat cattle eaten by seven lean ones, and no wise man in the empire could tell him what it meant. Joseph could: seven years of plenty, then seven of famine. Store the grain now. Pharaoh made him master of all Egypt, and when the famine came, every nation on earth came to Egypt to buy bread, and Egypt became the greatest power of its age because a shepherd's son in a stolen coat could see seven years ahead.

The Long Memory files the Coat among the great arms for that reason. It never struck a blow. But the mightiest empire of its wave was built on what its wearer saw.

### The Staff and the Sea

Empires that are built on one man's sight forget him. A new Pharaoh rose who did not remember Joseph, and Joseph's people became slaves in the land their ancestor had saved.

The arm that freed them was the plainest-looking weapon in this chronicle: a shepherd's staff. Moses carried it in the wilderness, and when the voice in the burning bush told him to throw it down, it became a serpent, and when he caught the serpent by the tail, it became a staff again.

Before Pharaoh, the staffs met in what the heralds count as the first duel of arms between two empires that the Long Memory holds in full. The staff of Moses' house was cast down and became a serpent. Pharaoh's sorcerers, whom later tradition names Jannes and Jambres, cast down their staffs, and those became serpents too. Then the serpent of Moses' house swallowed theirs.

Ten plagues followed. Then the chase to the sea.

At the edge of the water, with the chariots of Egypt behind them, the finest war machines of that age, Moses did not strike the sea. He stretched the **Staff of Moses** out over it. A wind blew all night, and the waters stood up like walls, and the people walked through on dry ground. When the chariots followed, the walls fell.

The greatest army of the age was gone in a morning. That is what the Long Memory means when it speaks of arms that change the balance of power: not a better sword, but an arm that makes the old kind of strength irrelevant.

### The New Metal

The next challenge to the eldest peoples came from the sea, and it came with a new art.

The Philistines arrived on the coast with iron, and they guarded the secret jealously. Their scrolls are lost, but the Hebrew scrolls remember the fact plainly: there was no smith found throughout all the land of Israel, for the Philistines said, lest they make swords or spears. If a farmer needed his plough sharpened, he had to go down to the Philistines and pay for the privilege.

This is the pattern the Long Memory sees again and again. The eldest peoples founded the great arms. The peoples who rose to challenge them did not try to steal those arms. They invented new ones. Iron was the first great invention of the challengers, and it nearly ended the Hebrew wave.

The old people answered with whatever was at hand.

Samson, bound with new ropes by his own countrymen and handed to the Philistines as a peace offering, broke the ropes like burnt flax, picked up the fresh jawbone of a donkey from the ground, and killed a thousand men with it at a place called Lehi. The **Jawbone of Samson** is bone against iron, the old world's answer to the new. When it was done, he was dying of thirst, and a spring broke open in the hollow of the ground to save him.

Then came the Philistines' champion, Goliath of Gath: a giant in a coat of scale armor, with a spear whose head was six hundred shekels of iron, a walking monument to the new metal. Every soldier of Israel was afraid of him. The shepherd boy David walked out against him without armor, because the king's armor did not fit, and killed him with a stone from a sling: the oldest weapon there is.

Then David took the giant's own sword and cut off his head with it.

The **Sword of Goliath** did not go home with David. It was wrapped in a cloth and laid up behind the ephod, the priest's sacred garment, in the sanctuary at Nob, as a trophy and a warning. Years later, when David was fleeing for his life from a jealous king, starving and unarmed, he came to Nob and asked the priest if there was any weapon there. The priest told him there was only one. David said: there is none like that. Give it to me.

### The Mantle

The last great arm of this wave was the strangest of all, because its power was inheritance itself.

The prophet Elijah called fire down from heaven on Mount Carmel to prove whose power was real. He wore a rough mantle of hair, and with it rolled up he struck the river Jordan, and the river parted, an echo of the Staff a wave earlier. His disciple Elisha followed him across, and Elijah asked him what he wanted before the end.

Elisha said: a double portion of your spirit.

Elijah said: that is a hard thing. But if you see me when I am taken, it will be yours.

Then a chariot of fire with horses of fire came between them, and Elijah went up in a whirlwind into heaven, and Elisha saw it. The **Mantle of Elijah** fell from the sky. Elisha picked it up, struck the Jordan as his master had, and the river parted for him too.

The heralds count this moment as the birth of an idea that runs through the rest of this chronicle: that arms do not merely belong to their makers, or their takers. They can seek an heir. They can choose.

The Hebrew wave crested on, and broke, as all waves do, in exile and in ash. Its arms scattered. Some were carried off by conquerors and never seen again. Some were hidden by priests who died with the secret. The Long Memory holds that none of them were destroyed.

## III. The Age of Labors

The Greeks were not the first wave, and, unusually, they knew it. Their own songs said that before their gods there were older powers, the Titans, and before the Titans older still. That honesty is rare enough that the heralds respect it. It is also why the Greek songs hold some of the clearest accounts of arms overthrowing an order.

### The Sickle and the Helm

The first sky-father, Ouranos, kept his children imprisoned in the depths of the earth. The earth herself, their mother, forged a great sickle of adamant, the unbreakable metal, and gave it to her youngest Titan son, Cronus. With it, he overthrew his father. That sickle is the first overthrow-weapon in the Greek memory. Remember it.

Cronus then ruled, and swallowed his own children to keep them from doing to him what he had done to his father. One escaped: Zeus. And when Zeus went to war against the Titans, the war went on for ten years, evenly matched, until Zeus went down into Tartarus and freed the prisoners there: the Cyclopes, master smiths, whom the Titans had chained.

In gratitude, the Cyclopes forged three arms. A thunderbolt for Zeus. A trident for Poseidon. And for Hades, a helm that made its wearer invisible: the **Helm of Darkness**.

Ten years, the Titans had held. Once the new arms were forged, they fell. The Long Memory sets this beside the Churning and the Red Sea. A balance that held for an age broke the moment one side had better arms.

### The New Way: Labor

After the Titan war, something changed in the Greek wave that the heralds consider one of the great turns in the whole history of arms.

The eldest peoples got their arms from heaven: given, granted, grown. The Greek heroes got theirs by labor. They were mortals, mostly, or half-mortals, and they took their arms from monsters, by strength, by trickery, by endurance. This was the challengers' second great invention, after iron. Not a new metal, but a new kind of wielder: one who had to earn it.

Perseus was sent to bring back the head of the Gorgon Medusa, whose gaze turned anyone who met it to stone. The gods equipped him with arms from the oldest wars. Hermes gave him the **Harpe**, a sickle-sword of adamant, which some of the Long Memory's keepers hold to be the very sickle Cronus used to overthrow his father, or forged from the same metal. From the nymphs he borrowed the Helm of Darkness. From Athena he took a shield polished like a mirror. He crept up on Medusa unseen, looked only at her reflection, and took her head with one stroke of the sickle.

Two arms of the Titan war, carried by one mortal against one monster. The heralds call that the moment the gods' arms passed into human hands.

Heracles went further. His first labor was the Nemean Lion, a child of Typhon, the last and greatest monster the old powers sent against the gods. Its hide could not be cut. Heracles' arrows bounced off it. His sword bent. So he cornered it in its own cave and strangled it with his bare hands, and when even then he could not skin it, he used its own claws to do it. He wore the **Nemean Lion's Pelt** from then on, as armor no blade could pass.

For his ninth labor, he was sent for the war belt of Hippolyta, queen of the Amazons, a gift to her from Ares, god of war. The Long Memory notes that she would have given it freely. She came down to his ship and offered it. But Hera, who hated Heracles, went among the Amazons in disguise and whispered that the stranger had come to carry off their queen. The Amazons attacked. In the confusion, Hippolyta died, and the **Girdle of Hippolyta** was taken in blood that did not need to be shed. A lesson the heralds teach every apprentice: a relic offered freely is worth more than one won, and suspicion has broken more alliances than swords.

Heracles also sailed, for a while, on the Argo, the ship of heroes that Jason took to Colchis at the edge of the world for the **Golden Fleece**. The Fleece hung in a grove sacred to Ares, guarded by a dragon that never slept. Jason yoked the king's fire-breathing bulls, sowed a field with dragon's teeth and survived the warriors that grew from it, but he did not take the Fleece by strength. The king's daughter Medea, a sorceress, charmed the sleepless dragon to sleep with her songs and her herbs. The Fleece was the right to a kingdom. It made Jason a king, and it made Medea a traitor to her own father, and both of those debts were paid, later, in terrible ways.

### The Sundering of Troy

Every wave ends in a Sundering, and the Greek wave's Sundering had a name everyone still knows: Troy.

Ten years the war went on, and the arms in it were the finest the heroes had ever carried. When Achilles refused to fight, his friend Patroclus put on Achilles' armor to frighten the Trojans, and the Trojan prince Hector killed him and stripped the armor from his body. Achilles' mother, the sea-nymph Thetis, went to the smith of the gods, Hephaestus, and asked for new armor.

Hephaestus made the **Armor of Achilles**, and on its shield he wrought the whole world: earth, sky and sea, the sun and moon and stars, a city at peace with weddings and lawsuits and dancing, a city at war under siege, ploughmen and reapers and a vineyard, and around all of it the great river Ocean. It was armor with the world on it, and Achilles wore it to kill Hector before the walls of Troy.

The Long Memory holds that Hector's own sword did not stay in the dust. Remember that as well.

Achilles died soon after, an arrow in his heel. Then the Greeks held a contest for his armor: it would go to the greatest of the heroes still living. Ajax, the giant, the shield-wall, who had carried Achilles' body off the field, claimed it by deeds. Odysseus claimed it by words. Odysseus won.

Ajax went mad with shame, and when he came back to himself, he fell on his own sword. It was a sword Hector had given him years before, after the two of them had fought to a draw and parted with gifts.

A suit of armor broke the greatest friendship of the age. Troy burned soon after, and the Greek wave broke with it, and its heroes scattered across the sea, and most of them never got home.

## IV. Iron and Storm

In the north and west, the next wave learned what the Philistines knew and something more. The Philistines had a new metal. These peoples had the forge and the rune, and they understood that an arm could be made with its price written into it. Their arms almost always came with a curse, a condition, or a bargain. That was their invention: arms that bound their bearers.

### The Silver Hand and the Answerer

In Ireland, the Long Memory holds the story of the Tuatha Dé Danann, a people of power who came from the islands of the north with four treasures: a stone that cried out under a true king, a spear that could not lose a battle, a sword that no one escaped, and a cauldron no company ever left hungry.

In the First Battle of Mag Tuired, against the people who held Ireland before them, their king Nuada lost his hand to a warrior's blade. They won the battle. But their law said that a king with a blemish could not rule, so Nuada gave up the throne, and it went to Bres the Beautiful, half of the Fomorians, the sea-raiders who were the Dé Danann's oldest enemies.

Bres was a tyrant. He taxed the people until their chimneys did not smoke, and set their great men to digging ditches, and gave no feasts. Poets satirized him until boils rose on his face.

The physician Dian Cecht, with the wright Credne, made Nuada a new hand of silver that moved like flesh: **Airgetlam**. With it, Nuada was whole in the eyes of the law, and he took his throne back from Bres without a single blow struck. Then Dian Cecht's own son Miach did better, and grew Nuada a hand of real flesh. Dian Cecht, in jealousy, killed his son for it. The heralds note that even a healing-arm has a price.

Bres fled to his Fomorian kin and raised a war against the Dé Danann. And then a stranger came to the gates of Tara.

The doorkeeper asked him what skill he brought, for no one entered without one. He said: I am a smith. We have a smith, said the doorkeeper. I am a harper. We have a harper. A warrior, a poet, a healer, a sorcerer, a craftsman. We have all of those. Then ask your king, said the stranger, whether he has one man who has all of these skills at once.

That was Lugh of the Long Arm, and he carried **Fragarach, the Answerer**, a sword from the Land of Promise across the sea, which he had from Manannan mac Lir, lord of the waves. No armor stopped it. No wind blew against its bearer. And it was said that no one at its point could lie, or move.

At the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, the Fomorian king Balor opened his evil eye, whose gaze killed armies, and it was Nuada of the Silver Hand who fell before it. Lugh killed Balor with a stone from a sling through that eye, the second time in this chronicle that the oldest weapon in the world felled the champion of a new power.

### The Spear From the Ford

A later crest of that same people produced the hero Cú Chulainn of Ulster, who trained with the warrior-woman Scáthach on her island of shadows. She taught her students everything, but one weapon she taught to only one: the **Gae Bolg**, made from the bone of a sea-beast that died fighting another sea-beast. It was cast with the foot, from the water of a ford. It entered as one barb and opened into thirty inside the body. It never failed.

With it, Cú Chulainn held the ford against the whole army of Connacht in the great Cattle Raid of Cooley. And with it, he killed his own foster-brother Ferdiad, who had been forced to fight him at that ford, and his own son Connla, whom he did not recognize until it was too late.

A weapon that cannot miss, in the hand of a hero who cannot stop. It won the war. It took everything he loved.

### The Wager of the Forges

In the far north, the Long Memory holds the story of the Aesir, whom your songs call the gods of Asgard, and whom the heralds count simply as one of the mightiest peoples of their wave.

Their wave began with a war. The Aesir and the Vanir, two peoples of power, fought what their own songs call the first war in the world, and it began when Odin cast his spear over the Vanir host. To throw the spear over an army was to give that army to Odin. It is a gesture men repeated in the north for a thousand years after.

That spear was **Gungnir**, and it came from a wager.

Loki, the trickster, had cut off the golden hair of Thor's wife Sif as a prank, and to save his own life he went to the dwarves called the sons of Ivaldi and had them make her new hair of real gold. While their forge was hot, they made two more things: a ship that could be folded into a pocket, and a spear that never missed its mark.

Loki, pleased with himself, then bet his own head that no dwarves anywhere could make three treasures as fine. The brothers Brokkr and Eitri took the bet. Eitri worked the forge, and Brokkr worked the bellows, and Loki, in the shape of a fly, bit Brokkr again and again to make him stop. On the last treasure, the fly bit Brokkr on the eyelid, and blood ran into his eye, and he let go of the bellows for a moment to wipe it away.

The last treasure was a hammer: **Mjolnir**. Because of that moment, its handle came out short. It was still judged the finest of all six treasures, because it was the one thing that could hold back the giants.

Two forges, one wager, and the balance of the nine worlds was settled for an age.

### The Hammer, the Belt and the Gloves

The giants did try. Once, the giant king Thrym stole Mjolnir while Thor slept and buried it eight leagues under the earth, and said he would give it back only if the goddess Freyja were given to him as a bride.

Loki borrowed Freyja's falcon cloak, **Valshamr**, and flew to the giants' realm to learn where the hammer was. The answer settled nothing: Freyja refused, in a rage that shook her halls. So Thor himself went to the wedding, wearing a bridal veil and Freyja's necklace, with Loki dressed as his bridesmaid. The bride ate an ox and eight salmon at the feast. Loki explained that she had not eaten for eight nights, so eager was she for the wedding. And when Thrym ordered the hammer brought out and laid in the bride's lap to bless the marriage, Thor took it back, and that was the end of Thrym's hall.

The same falcon cloak, on another day, carried Loki to the hall of the giant Thiazi, who had stolen Idunn and her apples of youth. Without the apples, the gods were growing old. Loki turned Idunn into a nut, took her in his claws, and flew home, with Thiazi chasing in the shape of an eagle. The gods lit a fire on the walls of Asgard, and the eagle's wings burned.

When Thor went, unarmed, to the hall of the fire giant Geirrod, a giantess named Grid warned him of the trap and lent him three things: a staff, a belt of strength, and a pair of iron gloves. Geirrod hurled a lump of red-hot iron at him across the hall. Thor caught it in **Jarngreipr**, the iron gloves, and threw it back through the pillar Geirrod was hiding behind, and through Geirrod. The belt was **Megingjord**, and buckled on, it doubled the strength of whoever wore it. From then on, the songs say, Thor never went to war without all three: the hammer, the belt, and the gloves.

### The Cursed Blade

Not every great arm of the north belonged to the gods. The kings of men had theirs too, and theirs were worse.

King Svafrlami caught two dwarves outside their stone and would not let them go until they forged him a sword with a golden hilt that would never rust, that would cut iron like cloth, and that would always win. They made it. As they handed it over, they cursed it: **Tyrfing** would kill a man every time it was drawn, it would do three great evils, and it would be the death of Svafrlami himself. All three came true.

It passed down through the berserker Angantyr, who was buried with it in a barrow on the island of Samsø. His daughter Hervor went alone to the burial mounds at night, among the ghost-fires, and called on her dead father to give her the sword. He refused. He warned her it would destroy her line. She would not leave without it. At last the dead man threw it to her from the grave.

Generations later, Tyrfing decided the greatest war the north remembers, the Battle of the Goths and the Huns, when a king killed his own half-brother with it on the field. The dwarves had promised it would always win. They never said for whom.

### Gold and the Dragon

The Long Memory holds that the spear Gungnir once broke another great sword. The hero Sigmund carried Gram, a sword Odin himself had set in a tree for the worthiest to pull out, and in his last battle an old one-eyed man in a grey cloak stood in his way and held out a spear, and Gram shattered on it. The Wanderer, whom everyone knew and no one named, had decided Sigmund's time was over.

Gram was reforged for Sigmund's son Sigurd, and with it Sigurd killed the dragon Fafnir. Fafnir had once been a man who killed his own father for a hoard of cursed dwarf-gold. He had become a dragon to guard it, and he wore on his brow **Aegishjalmur**, the Helm of Awe, which struck terror into everyone who looked on him. Sigurd did not face that terror. On advice he did not fully trust, and on better advice from a stranger with one eye, he dug a pit in the path where the dragon went down to drink, hid in it, and struck upward into the one place the dragon was soft.

He took the helm, and the hoard, and the curse with it.

### Ragnarok

The Sundering of the north had a name, and the north had been waiting for it the whole time: Ragnarok, the twilight of the powers.

At the end, the wolf Fenrir broke his chains. The giants came over the rainbow bridge. Odin rode out with Gungnir, the spear that never misses, and it did not save him. Fenrir swallowed him whole.

That is the third great lesson of the Long Memory, after the price of the Chakra and the absence of the Kavacha: no arm, however great, is greater than the wave it belongs to. When the wave breaks, even a spear that never misses misses.

But Odin's son Vidar was there, and on one foot he wore a shoe of unusual thickness, stitched, the songs say, from every scrap of leather that every shoemaker since the beginning of the world had cut away and thrown out. He set that foot on the wolf's lower jaw, took the upper jaw in his hands, and tore Fenrir apart. The **Shoe of Vidar** was made by no god, from nothing anyone wanted. The heralds find that worth remembering.

Thor killed the world-serpent and walked nine steps before its venom killed him. When the world rose again from the sea, green and new, his sons Modi and Magni found Mjolnir lying in the grass, and picked it up.

Even at the end of all things, the hammer found an heir.

## V. The Eastern Tides

While the northern wave was cresting and breaking, other waves moved across the world, and the Long Memory notes something curious about them. Every wave remembers a flood. The wave of the great rivers remembers one. The Hebrew wave remembers one. The Greek wave remembers one. The heralds have long suspected that it was the same flood, remembered from different shores.

### The Pillar of the Sea

In the far East, the flood came in the age of the sage-kings, and it did not recede. For thirteen years, a man named Yu worked to tame it, cutting channels, dredging rivers, leading the waters to the sea. Three times in those years he passed the door of his own house and did not go in. To measure the depth of the waters, he used an iron pillar, and when the waters were tamed at last, the pillar was left on the floor of the Eastern Sea to anchor the tides. Yu founded the first dynasty of that land. An arm used for no war at all had founded an empire.

The pillar lay in the undersea palace of Ao Guang, Dragon King of the Eastern Sea, for ages after. It weighed thirteen thousand five hundred jin, and no one could lift it.

Until the Monkey King, Sun Wukong, born from a stone and grown arrogant on his own power, came to the dragon's palace looking for a weapon worthy of him, and found that the pillar answered him. He told it to grow smaller, and it did. **Ruyi Jingu Bang**, the as-you-will gold-banded staff, could be as tall as heaven or as small as a needle, and he kept it behind his ear.

With it, he wrote his own name off the registers of death, ate the peaches of immortality, and fought all the armies of heaven to a standstill. He was not stopped by any arm. He was stopped by a palm. The Buddha wagered that Sun Wukong could not leap out of his hand, and Sun Wukong leapt to what he thought was the end of the world and found five great pillars there, and wrote his name on one. Then he looked down and saw he was standing in the Buddha's palm, and the five pillars were fingers. He was sealed under a mountain for five hundred years, and when he came out, he was a different being, and he spent the rest of his days protecting a monk on a long road west.

The pillar that settled the flood unsettled heaven. The Long Memory pairs it with the Staff of Moses: two arms that commanded the sea, and two peoples that crossed water to found something new.

### The Grass-Cutter

On the islands farther east, the storm god Susanoo was banished from heaven for his violence against his sister, the sun goddess Amaterasu. Wandering on earth, he came upon an old couple weeping. An eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent, Yamata no Orochi, so huge that it stretched over eight valleys and eight hills, had eaten seven of their eight daughters, one each year, and it was coming for the last.

Susanoo did not fight the serpent head-on. He had eight vats of strong wine brewed and set behind a fence with eight gates. The serpent put a head into each vat and drank, and fell asleep, and Susanoo cut it to pieces. When his sword struck the middle tail, it chipped on something hard. He cut the tail open and found a sword inside it, and he knew at once that it was too great a thing to keep. He gave it to his sister, as an apology. A storm god and a sun goddess were reconciled by a sword.

Many generations later, the prince Yamato Takeru carried that sword east, and his enemies lured him onto an open plain and set the grass on fire around him. He cut the grass away with the sword, and the wind turned and drove the fire back on them. From then on it was called **Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi**, the Grass-Cutting Sword, and it became one of the three treasures of that land's emperors.

It is said that at the sea battle of Dan-no-ura, where the old ruling house of those islands fell, the grandmother of the child emperor took him in her arms, and took the sword, and stepped into the sea, and told him there was a capital beneath the waves.

The sea gave Ruyi Jingu Bang. The sea took Kusanagi. The Long Memory notes that the sea keeps what it takes only for a while.

## VI. Oaths and Hoards

After the old powers fell silent in the north and west, a wave rose whose strength was neither divine gift nor labor nor rune. Its strength was the oath and the hoard. Kings bound men to them with gold, and men bound themselves to kings with words, and the great arms of that wave were caught up in both.

### The Nibelung Gold

The dragon Fafnir's hoard had a long tail of its own. It began when Loki killed an otter that was really the son of a sorcerer, and to pay the blood-price he robbed the dwarf Andvari of all his gold, down to a single ring Andvari begged to keep. Loki took the ring too, and Andvari cursed it: it would be the death of everyone who owned it.

It was. The gold passed to the sorcerer, whose son Fafnir killed him for it. It passed to Fafnir, whom Sigurd killed for it. It passed to Sigurd, who was murdered in his bed. It passed to the kings of the Burgundians, who sank it in the Rhine rather than give it up, and died rather than tell where.

In the southern telling of the same tale, the dwarf of the hoard was Alberich, lord of the Nibelungs, and the hoard held a helm, the **Tarnhelm**, that let its wearer take any shape, or none. Alberich was proud of it, and it undid him: tricksters asked him to show them what it could do, and he turned himself into a great serpent, and then, when they doubted he could do the opposite, into a toad. They put a foot on the toad.

Gold has no edge. The heralds hold that it has cut down more kings than any sword in this chronicle.

### The Sword With Other Ages in It

The last great arm of that wave belonged to Roland, the nephew of the emperor Charlemagne and the greatest of his twelve peers: **Durendal**.

The Long Memory gathers three things about it from three different keepers. The first: that in its golden hilt were set relics of the Hebrew wave and those that followed it: a tooth of the apostle Peter, blood of Saint Basil, hair of Saint Denis, a thread of the robe of Mary. The second: that it was unbreakable. The third, from an Italian keeper of songs: that before it was Roland's, it was Hector's. The sword of the Trojan prince, which had not stayed in the dust.

If that keeper is right, Durendal carried in it the Greek wave and the Hebrew wave both. It is the first arm in the Long Memory that the heralds call a braided relic: an arm that carries other ages inside it.

At the pass of Roncevaux, Charlemagne's rearguard was ambushed by an overwhelming army. Roland's friend Oliver begged him three times to blow his horn, the Olifant, to call the emperor's army back. Roland refused three times. He would not, he said, be shamed by calling for help. By the time he blew the horn, so hard that his temples burst, everyone around him was dead or dying.

Dying himself, Roland tried to break Durendal on a rock so that no enemy would ever carry it. He struck the rock ten times. The rock split. The sword did not. In the end he lay down on top of it, facing the enemy, so that the emperor would know he had died a conqueror.

There is a cliff at Rocamadour, in the south of that land, with a sword in it, and the people there will tell you it is Durendal, flung there by the dying Roland or carried there by an angel. The heralds have not pulled it out to check.

Roncevaux was the Sundering of the oath-wave, though it took centuries to finish breaking. It is the last Sundering in the Long Memory that the songs remember as a great clash of great arms. After it, the waves grew smaller.

## VII. The Hearth

Then came a long age that the heralds call the Hearth.

No empire held the great arms. They lay where the last waves had dropped them: in barrows, in hoards, in riverbeds, at the bottom of the sea, in cliffs. The gods were silent. The heroes were songs. And into that silence, the common folk stepped with an invention of their own: not a new metal, not a new kind of wielder, not a binding rune, but cunning. The magic of people who had nothing.

They learned, for instance, to make a **Hand of Glory**. You take the hand of a hanged man, cut from the gallows at midnight, and dry it and pickle it, and fix in its fingers a candle made from the hanged man's own fat. When it is lit, no one in a house wakes, and anyone who sees it stands frozen. Thieves carried them. Only milk will put the flame out. It is a horrible thing, and it is the only great arm in this chronicle that was made by a person with no power at all.

They learned to take what the great left lying around. Hop-o'-My-Thumb was the smallest of seven brothers, so small at birth he was no bigger than a thumb, abandoned in the forest by parents who could not feed him. He led his brothers to the house of an ogre who ate children, and by a trick he got the ogre to murder his own daughters instead, and he got his brothers out. When the ogre chased them in his **Seven-League Boots**, which covered seven leagues in a single stride, he grew tired and fell asleep on a rock. Hop-o'-My-Thumb crept up and pulled the boots off his feet and put them on his own. They were fairy boots, so they shrank to fit him. A boy no bigger than a thumb crossed a kingdom in a morning.

The great arms were given by gods, taken from monsters, and forged with curses. These two were made by the desperate and stolen by the small. The Long Memory counts them no less. The heralds will tell you that the Hearth is the age in which the arms came closest to you.

## VIII. The Long Quiet and the Echoes

Here the Long Memory comes to what it believes, and not only what it records.

The heralds believe that arms outlast their wielders, and that when a wave breaks, its arms do not end. They sink, and they wait. A spear that never misses waits in the grass. A sword waits in a cliff. A helm waits in a hoard. A wheel waits in the hand of a god who has stopped answering prayers.

They believe that souls outlast their waves as well. No people that has crested once is finished cresting. Its courage, its genius, its grudges, come back wearing new faces in new places. The heralds call those returning souls the **Echoes**. An Echo does not know what it is. It knows only that some stories hit harder than they should, and that some names sound like its own.

And they believe that arms and Echoes seek each other. That is why a sword will refuse a hundred hands and answer one. That is why a hammer rises for one person when giants have strained at it and failed. The relic is not choosing a stranger. It is recognizing someone.

Look back over this chronicle, and you will see that the balance of the world has never been settled by numbers, or by gold alone, or by the size of a kingdom. It has been settled again and again by a handful of arms in the right hands at the right hour:

- A wheel that decided who would drink the nectar of deathlessness.
- An armor given away, and a war lost because of it.
- A coat that saw seven years ahead and built an empire.
- A staff that drowned the finest army of its age.
- A new metal that nearly ended an old people, answered by a jawbone and a stone.
- Three arms forged in Tartarus that ended a ten-year war in a day.
- A suit of armor that broke the greatest friendship of the Greek wave.
- A silver hand that unseated a tyrant without a blow.
- A wager between two forges that settled nine worlds.
- A cursed sword that always won, for someone.
- A spear that never missed, until the night it did.
- An iron pillar that tamed a flood and then fought heaven.
- A sword found in a serpent's tail, given away as an apology.
- A sword with other ages in it, that a dying man could not break.
- A boy no bigger than a thumb, in boots too big for him.

The heralds have kept this memory through every Sundering since, for one purpose. Someone must be watching when the arms begin to wake.

---

## Epilogue: The Unnamed Age

They are waking now.

You will have noticed that the threats are stirring. Every day, something comes over the hills: a beast from one shore of memory, a tyrant from another, a dragon from a third. The heralds have never seen so many returning at once, from so many waves. Some of the court say the monsters are returning because the relics are waking. Some say the relics are waking because the monsters are returning. The heralds do not know which is the cause. They know only that it has happened before, at the start of every great age, and that each time, the age was named for what it won.

The court of the King is not an empire. It has no ancient name, no great river, no sacred mountain, no sea of its own. Most of its nobles began as peasants, in borrowed boots and no armor at all. It is, by every measure the heralds know, a Hearth folk.

But the Hearth is the age in which the arms came closest to ordinary hands. And the Long Memory is very clear that every wave began as something small.

Thirty relics of the old waves are stirring. Rumors of them are already reaching the court: a hammer laid in a bride's lap, a fleece that hangs where nothing sleeps, a spear cast from the water of a ford, a sword that a dying man could not break. Each one waits for its Echo. Each one will be guarded, as each was guarded before, by something that remembers what it cost the last time.

Soon the herald will open the trials. Whoever answers will follow a rumor down an old road, make the choice the legend teaches, and face the guardian at the end. If they are worthy, the relic will rise for them, as it rose for the one before.

The court has not yet named its age.

Ages are named by what they win.
